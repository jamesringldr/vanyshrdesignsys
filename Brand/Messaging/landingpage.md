# Landing Page Structure

## Source

Blake Emal (@heyblake): *"If you sell a hard-to-explain product or a $5,000 service, don't lead w/ deliverables. Build your homepage in this order:"* — 21 steps: name the buyer in their own words → show the symptom → repeat the outcome → three failed alternatives → reframe the problem → cost of staying unclear → introduce the offer → mechanism in 3 steps → first usable artifact → longer-term change → two proof points → show the work → implementation details → cost comparison → remove the risk → repeatable language → obvious next step → stop selling.

## Audit verdict: viable with edits

The logic is sound — it's the standard hard-offer playbook (buyer → symptom → outcome → failed alternatives → reframe → mechanism → proof → risk reversal), and steps 1–3 are literally our VoC translation rule ("users supply the promise language"). Three things need surgery for Vanyshr:

1. **It's built for $5K services, not $100/yr self-serve.** 21 sections is a sales-call page. Same beats, compressed to ~10 sections for a consumer attention budget.
2. **Service-coaching language doesn't port.** Step 7 ("the issue is not effort, it's positioning") and step 8 ("cost of staying unclear") need rewriting in Vanyshr terms.
3. **It's missing the trust beat.** The framework has no step for *"why should I give YOU my data?"* — our #1 cross-cutting objection (privacy paradox). For this category, that's load-bearing. Added as its own section.
4. **Pre-launch proof problem.** Steps 13–14 assume real customers. Vanyshr has none yet — proof substitutes only (sample receipts, methodology, the scan itself). **No fake testimonials, ever.** Explicit rule.
5. **The scan moves early.** Emal puts the first usable artifact at step 11. For Vanyshr the scan *is* the conversion engine (pair #5: urgency from your data) — it belongs right after the hero.

## Does Vanyshr qualify as "hard to explain"? Yes.

- **Re-removal** is non-obvious — buyers assume removal is one-time.
- **Synthetic obfuscation** needs teaching — nobody's heard of it, and it's the differentiator.
- **The agentic pipeline is invisible by design** — you can't demo what the user never touches.
- **The category is poisoned by distrust** — so the failed-alternatives + proof architecture isn't optional, it's the sale.
- Lead with deliverables ("we remove you from 200 brokers") and you sound like every competitor. The framework's core injunction holds.

## The structure — 10 sections

### 1. Hero (Emal 1–3)
Buyer in their own words, recognized symptom, wanted outcome. No deliverables.
- Headline in VoC: *"Get your info off the internet — and keep it off."*
- Sub carries the enterprise frame: enterprise-grade defense, made simple, priced for individuals and families.
- Single CTA: free scan. (A/B: enterprise-frame vs. approachable-frame.)

### 2. The Scan (Emal 11, moved early; Emal 2 embodied; Emal 8 folded in)
*"Show what's out there."* Interactive scan → findings risk-tagged **Scam / Spam / Fraud / Hack**, each with a one-line "how this gets used against you" tooltip.
- Urgency from your data, not our adjectives. Calm copy, alarming facts.
- The cost of staying exposed is shown here, not told — the risk tags do the work.

### 3. Failed alternatives (Emal 4–6)
Three, in "noticeably better" tone — never trashing:
1. **DIY opt-outs** — works (Consumer Reports: manual beat paid services), costs 200+ hours you don't have.
2. **One-time removal services** — the data comes back. Category complaint #1.
3. **Monitoring-only** — watches your data, doesn't remove it. (The Carefull/Allstate lane.)
- Close: the issue isn't effort — it's that removal doesn't stick, and what survives stays trustworthy. (Emal 7, rewritten.)

### 4. The mechanism in 3 steps (Emal 10; Emal 16 merged in, kept light)
The five S's as the visual; three steps as the explainer:
1. **Show** — we sweep the brokers and show you everything.
2. **Shred** — we remove your profiles, handled entirely behind the scenes.
3. **Stuff + Shadow** — we seed synthetic data so what's left becomes unreliable, and we stand watch — anything that comes back gets re-removed automatically.
- Consumer-light on implementation. The pipeline detail lives one click deeper, not on the homepage.

### 5. Trust beat (added — Emal's gap)
*"Why should you give us your data?"* The privacy paradox, answered head-on:
- Inability-framed: what we **can't** see, sell, or share — not just what we won't.
- What we collect, why we need it, how to delete everything.
- No dark patterns, ever.

### 6. Proof (Emal 13–15, pre-launch substitutes)
*"Receipts, not counters."* Show the work:
- Annotated sample removal receipt + re-removal log entry.
- Honest-counter methodology: what we count, what we never count.
- **Rule: zero fabricated testimonials.** Until real customers exist, the artifact is the proof.

### 7. Cost (Emal 17)
$100/yr vs. the alternatives: 200+ DIY hours, competitor pricing, the cost of a breach or a scam that lands.
- Frame: enterprise-grade protection at a consumer price — the "steal" perception, stated plainly.

### 8. Risk reversal (Emal 18)
*"We stand behind every removal."* One-click cancellation, what happens to your data when you leave, no retention hostage-taking.

### 9. Repeatable language (Emal 19)
The one-liner they tell their spouse / group chat: *"Vanyshr gets my info off the internet and keeps it off."* Built for referral.

### 10. Next step + stop (Emal 20–21)
Obvious CTA: run your free scan. Then stop selling — confident close, no urgency widgets, no countdown timers.

## A/B test variants (from the workshop)
- Enterprise-frame vs. approachable-frame hero (cluster 1 decision).
- Surveillance-frame ("tired of being tracked?") vs. standard-frame (Flock-mood test).
- "Reclaim your data" vs. "Secure your data" umbrella CTA.
