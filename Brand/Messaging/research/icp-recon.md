# ICP Recon — Who actually buys data-removal products

> **What this is:** evidence-backed ideal-customer-profile research for Vanyshr brand messaging. Ranked buyer hypotheses with cited sources, willingness-to-pay signals, congregation points, and an honest read on the family-plan hypothesis. · **Created:** 2026-09-24 · **Status:** draft — for workshop input, not a market-sizing study.

## Method & caveats

- Sources: web search (reviews, comparisons, guides), social listening across Facebook/Instagram/Threads (a ~130KB corpus of posts + comment summaries about DeleteMe/Incogni/Cloaked), Trustpilot ratings via comparison sites, YouTube reviewer tests.
- **Reddit could not be read directly** (fetch blocked); Reddit sentiment below comes via secondhand summaries on affiliate/comparison sites — flagged where used. Treat those as directional, not verbatim.
- Many YouTube/comparison sources use affiliate links (discount codes, bit.ly links). Their conclusions skew toward "X is worth it" — but their *user quotes, pricing data, and test methodology* are still usable evidence.
- No invented statistics. Where data is thin, it's labeled thin.

## Ranked ICP hypotheses

### ICP-1 — Harassment / stalking survivors & people in domestic-safety situations
**Trigger event:** being stalked, harassed, threatened, or leaving an abusive partner — the moment someone realizes their home address is findable online.

**Buyer-propensity evidence (strongest in the set):**
- A woman creator shared: she "rarely discusses it online, but years ago she had a stalker who started with messages and ended by showing up at her home after finding her address through data broker sites" (social corpus, Facebook/Instagram).
- A former Manhattan public defender shared being "stalked after someone used publicly available data broker information to locate her home" (social corpus).
- Comment-level proof of the trigger: "He found out where I lived and kept driving by." (social corpus, verbatim comment).
- Privacy educator Caitlin Sarian (@cybersecuritygirl, Instagram reel, 4K likes / 1.1K comments) frames broker exposure as physical safety, not privacy: "anyone online can find your exact address for less than $5" and "stalkers, exes, or strangers could locate your home, phone, and family" (social corpus).
- A data-broker removal guide states it plainly: "Abusers, stalkers, and harassers routinely use people-search websites to find their victims' home addresses, phone numbers, and the names of their relatives. For domestic violence survivors, a single publicly listed address can be the difference between safety and danger." — https://dmca512.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/HOW-TO-REMOVE-YOUR-REAL-INFORMATION-FROM-DATA-BROKER-SITES.pdf
- Review guidance: DeleteMe is "likely worth it if… You've experienced stalking or harassment" — https://en.paperblog.com/is-deleteme-legit-in-2026-honest-review-with-safety-check-and-results-8066131/

**Where to find them:** domestic-violence support orgs and forums, stalking-victim resources, true-crime/creator communities (the stalker-story creators above), privacy-educator audiences (cybersecuritygirl-type accounts). Note: this is a sensitive audience — fear-based marketing to them is exploitative; the honest frame is safety utility.

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** highest urgency = highest willingness; this segment buys on *safety*, not privacy abstraction. No price resistance surfaced in this segment's content (they ask "does it work," not "is it too expensive").

### ICP-2 — Identity-theft & fraud victims / the breach-fatigued
**Trigger event:** identity stolen, card fraud, or the Nth "your data was breached" notice. The buyer has felt the cost.

**Buyer-propensity evidence:**
- A YouTube reviewer who tested six services with real PII: "I've had my identity stolen twice. Both times, what caught it wasn't a service that scrubbed my name off some site — it was one that warned me before the damage was done." — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sr8zVx9pYFU (affiliate-linked; quote is the reviewer's own framing)
- ZDNet's reviewer: data removal services are "well worth it — especially if you've been caught in multiple data breaches" — https://static.obstracts.com/feed/7759be08-34bc-5e54-b446-f6ec22310a1c/posts/951f8559-ffdc-5d87-b291-ba8c2d7c6177/951f8559-ffdc-5d87-b291-ba8c2d7c6177_a-data-removal-service-helped-me.pdf
- Aura's competitive page cites "Americans lost over $12.8 billion to fraud and identity theft last year alone" as the category's fear anchor — http://www.aura.com/learn/incogni-vs-deleteme (Aura's number; treat as their marketing stat, not verified)
- Trustpilot reviewers of Incogni (4.4/5, 2,029 reviews) "consistently praising its effectiveness in reducing spam and improving their personal cybersecurity… a significant decrease in unwanted calls and emails" — https://nordvpn.com/blog/incogni-vs-deleteme/ (Trustpilot data via NordVPN, Aug 2025)
- DeleteMe Trustpilot reviewers "frequently commend its effectiveness… Many users highlight feeling safer after using the service, noting a noticeable reduction in spam emails and unsolicited calls" — https://nordvpn.com/blog/incogni-vs-deleteme/

**Where to find them:** identity-theft support communities and subreddits, personal-finance forums (post-fraud threads), breach-notification moments (partner with the "you've been breached" trigger), YouTube review ecosystem.

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** buys on *loss avoidance*; the $100/yr price reads as cheap against fraud-cleanup costs (one family-identity-theft video cites "330 hours of cleanup calls" as the real cost — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gDVnQHJXd8M, affiliate-linked).

### ICP-3 — Adult children buying for elderly parents (scam-protection buyers)
**Trigger event:** a parent gets scammed, or the buyer discovers how much of mom/dad is listed on people-search sites.

**Buyer-propensity evidence:**
- Cult of Mac review: "Data scrubber saved my aging relative from scams" — the reviewer's relative had broker profiles including "links to profiles of her late husband, her kids and more." Key mechanism: "Scammers glean personal details from data brokers, and the info helps them tailor their scams to make them more convincing." — https://www.cultofmac.com/reviews/incogni-review
- This is a *gift/purchase-on-behalf* motion — the buyer is not the user. Different funnel: the adult child pays, the parent is enrolled.

**Where to find them:** elder-fraud prevention content, AARP-adjacent communities, "how to protect aging parents from scams" search traffic, Facebook (older-demo + family-gifting behavior).

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** buys on *protecting someone else*; price sensitivity lower when framed as elder safety. No direct price quotes surfaced for this segment — thin on WTP specifics.

### ICP-4 — Privacy-pragmatist techies / DIY graduates (time-vs-money buyers)
**Trigger event:** trying the DIY opt-out grind, hitting the wall of 100+ broker forms, and deciding their time is worth more.

**Buyer-propensity evidence:**
- "For €7.49/month annually, Incogni saved me approximately 20-25 hours of manual broker work over the past 12 weeks. That's worth €100+ to me even at minimum wage equivalents." — https://DigitalShieldPro.com/posts/incogni-review-2026/
- "Considering the amount of time it takes to manually remove your own data (40-80 hours across multiple data brokers), spending $100 to buy back that time is a great deal." — https://www.allthingssecured.com/reviews/data-removal/deleteme/
- A DIY opt-out guide concedes the ceiling: "This guide walks you through finding your listings, submitting opt-outs the right way… treat it as a habit you repeat once or twice a year rather than a one-time fix" — https://makingsenseofsecurity.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/msos-howto-opt-out-of-data-broker-people-search-sites-full.pdf (the DIY funnel is real and large — GitHub hosts a 400-site opt-out guide: https://github.com/thumpersecure/opt-out-manual-2026)
- Hacker News thread on deleting data from brokers shows the split: some insist on DIY hardening, others concede "There's no way you can delete your information from all of them without subscribing to a service" — https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38990755
- Review guidance: DeleteMe is "likely worth it if… You value privacy but lack time"; "may not be worth it if… You're comfortable submitting opt-out forms yourself" — https://en.paperblog.com/is-deleteme-legit-in-2026-honest-review-with-safety-check-and-results-8066131/
- Optery's free tier + self-service funnel specifically catches this segment: "Free exposure reports and self-service removals" for "Extreme budget constraints ($3.99/month)", "Willingness to do manual removals", "Students and young professionals" — https://www.securityhero.io/best-data-broker-removal-service/

**Where to find them:** Hacker News, r/privacy, r/PrivacyGuides, GitHub DIY-guide audiences, privacy-tool YouTube. They comparison-shop hard and punish inflated claims (see "Negative themes" below).

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** the most price-sensitive buyer in the set. Anchors: ~$7-8/mo is "obviously worth it" (time arbitrage); $129/yr gets debated cent-by-cent ("DeleteMe works, but paying $129/year for what Incogni does better at $100/year makes zero sense" — https://vpntierlists.com/blog/optery-vs-deleteme-what-reddit-really-says-in-2026, affiliate site summarizing Reddit; directional only). Free-tier-to-paid is the proven on-ramp (Optery).

### ICP-5 — Doxxing-risk online voices (journalists, creators, activists, streamers)

**Buyer-propensity evidence:**
- A doxxing victim's guide: "Consider investing in services like DeleteMe: DeleteMe is a service that will help you remove personal information from search engines and other websites. You submit your personal information, they do a little searching for you, and you remove what you'd like." — https://www.honeywhippedfeta.com/article/doxxed-by-a-republican-news-site-tips-for-navigating-doxxing-and-protecting-your-digital-identity
- An election-security doxing guide lists "Consider professional help (e.g. DeleteMe, Brightlines, SocialScout)" — https://electionsgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Protect-yourself-from-Doxing.pdf

- Creator Pall (social corpus) promotes removal after "increased online harassment he has experienced, specifically stalking and attempts to hack his accounts" — harassment-as-trigger for visible online figures.

**Where to find them:** creator/streamer communities, journalism safety orgs (CPJ-adjacent resources), activist networks, post-doxxing support threads.

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** buys on *reputational/physical safety*; expenses it like insurance. Thin on direct price quotes.

### ICP-6 — High-risk professions (law, healthcare, journalism, public office)
**Trigger event:** professional exposure — clients, patients, litigants, or the public can find your home.

**Buyer-propensity evidence:**
- Review guidance lists "You work in a high-risk profession (law, healthcare, journalism)" as a core "worth it" criterion — https://en.paperblog.com/is-deleteme-legit-in-2026-honest-review-with-safety-check-and-results-8066131/
- The former-Manhattan-public-defender stalking case (ICP-1) is also this segment's cautionary tale — professional visibility created the attack surface (social corpus).

**Where to find them:** professional associations, legal/healthcare forums, employer benefit channels (this smells like a B2B2C play — employer-paid privacy benefit).

**Willingness-to-pay signal:** thin on direct quotes; likely expense-able or employer-subsidized, implying lower price sensitivity.

### ICP-7 — New parents / "protect the kids" households — EVIDENCE THIN
**What the evidence actually shows:** the family/household pitch in-market is **child identity monitoring (SSN/breach), not data-broker removal**:
- Aura Family ($30/mo or $300/yr, up to 5 adults + unlimited kids) leads with "child SSN monitoring," parental controls, "Safe Gaming" chat monitoring — the family sale is identity-theft protection for kids, with broker removal as one bundled line item — https://cyberinsider.com/data-removal/aura-data-removal-review/
- One family-identity-theft video: "Why Families Are the #1 Target… a credit history your child discovers at 18 when they try to open their first bank account" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gDVnQHJXd8M (affiliate-linked)
- DeleteMe's family plan (up to 4 people, $329/yr) is positioned on per-person savings ("$82.25/person/yr, 36% savings vs single") — a discount tier, not a lead offer — https://cyberinsider.com/data-removal/deleteme-review/
- Social commenters ask questions about family plans (pricing, how they work) — curiosity, not demand evidence (social corpus).
- **No evidence found** of any competitor leading with family as the primary offer, or of a ~10-user plan anywhere in the category.

**Verdict:** the "protect my kids" emotion is real but currently monetized through *child identity monitoring*, a different job than broker removal. Whether a generous family *removal* plan converts as a lead offer is unproven — see the family-plan assessment below.

## Where buyers congregate (ranked by signal density)

1. **r/privacy, r/VPN, r/cybersecurity** — the comparison-shopping arena; "247 Reddit comments analyzed" across these subs in one affiliate summary (directional — https://vpntierlists.com/blog/optery-vs-deleteme-what-reddit-really-says-in-2026). Buyers here punish inflated removal counts and reward proof.
2. **YouTube review ecosystem** — high-intent "is X worth it" traffic; heavily affiliate-driven (assume every verdict is monetized), but comment sections carry real buyer questions.
3. **Trustpilot** — Incogni 4.4/5 (2,029 reviews), DeleteMe 4.0–4.4/5 (~180–200 reviews). Review volume itself is a signal: Incogni's 10x review count suggests 10x the buyer base or a stronger review engine — https://nordvpn.com/blog/incogni-vs-deleteme/
4. **Facebook/Instagram/Threads creator audiences** — privacy educators (cybersecuritygirl: 4K likes/1.1K comments on one broker reel) and survivor-story creators drive discovery; comment sections show the objection set in buyers' own words (social corpus).
5. **Hacker News** — the DIY-or-buy debate; hostile to marketing, receptive to technical honesty — https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38990755
6. **DIY guide hubs** (GitHub opt-out guides, dmca512.com, makingsenseofsecurity.com) — the top of the DIY-graduate funnel (ICP-4). A presence here (honest guides, not ads) catches buyers at the moment DIY breaks down.
7. **Domestic-safety & victim-support orgs** — the ICP-1 channel; partnership/education motion, not ad targeting.

## Negative themes — the objections Vanyshr's copy must preempt

1. **"Does it stay deleted?"** — the relisting problem is the #1 skepticism driver. Social commenters ask "whether data stays deleted after canceling" (social corpus). Honest framing wins: "Data brokers rebuild your profile the moment you cancel" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aatvGPKbUE).
2. **"The info is free/public anyway"** — "Most of the time this information is free and public" (verbatim social comment). Counter: public records are the *source*; the product removes the *aggregated, searchable* exposure.
3. **Inflated removal counts destroy trust** — "One tester's Incogni account showed over 771 reported removals — meant to alarm, not reassure" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sr8zVx9pYFU). Vanyshr's quantified-proof lane must count *verified* removals or it inherits this distrust.
4. **"It didn't do anything"** — "I got to leave me on the suggestion of an influencer and it has not helped me at all!" (verbatim social comment). Expectation-setting at signup is the fix.
5. **DeleteMe Trustpilot negatives:** info remaining in some databases post-removal; support responsiveness (weekends); technical issues with masked emails/custom requests — https://nordvpn.com/blog/incogni-vs-deleteme/
6. **Incogni Trustpilot negatives:** mixed support experiences; "doubts about its overall impact on spam activity… results varied" — https://nordvpn.com/blog/incogni-vs-deleteme/
7. **"What if the removal service gets hacked?"** — "But, do they bank your info? And, what if they get hacked?" (verbatim social comment). This is exactly where inability-framed trust copy ("we literally cannot access your data") earns its keep.

## Honest assessment: the family-plan hypothesis

**James's hypothesis:** lead with a generous family plan (~10 users) as the primary offer, on the theory that competitors treat family as a throwaway add-on and there's an open lane.

**What the evidence actually says:**

1. **Nobody leads with family.** Every competitor positions family as a tiered upsell: DeleteMe Family (4 people, $329/yr, sold on per-person discount), Incogni Family (up to 5), Aura Family (5 adults + unlimited kids, $300/yr). Family is always step 2 after the individual plan — never the hero offer. That *could* mean open lane, or it could mean nobody's found demand there. The evidence doesn't distinguish; treat "open lane" as unproven, not as validation.

2. **The family pitch that works in-market is child identity monitoring, not broker removal.** Aura — the only brand that truly leads on family — sells "child SSN monitoring," parental controls, and kid identity theft insurance. Broker removal is a bundled line item. A 10-user *removal* plan is a different product story than the one family buyers are currently shown.

3. **No ~10-user plan exists in the category.** Caps observed: 4 (DeleteMe), 5 (Incogni, Aura). A 10-user lead offer would be 2x the most generous cap in-market. Novel — but novel means untested pricing psychology ("why 10? who has 10?") and untested unit economics.

4. **The unit-economics risk is real and specific.** Broker removal is operationally expensive per enrolled identity (scrapes, submissions, follow-ups, re-checks — the work scales per person, not per account). A generous 10-user plan multiplies cost per subscription while the price anchor in-category sits at $100–130/yr for *one* person. Unless Vanyshr's agentic pipeline genuinely collapses per-user marginal cost, generosity here is a margin trap, not a moat.

5. **One supporting crumb:** a reviewer lists "Family with shared address (great family plan)" as an Incogni good-fit case — https://DigitalShieldPro.com/posts/incogni-review-2026/ — and social commenters ask about family plans. Curiosity exists. Demand as a *lead* offer does not have evidence.

**Bottom line:** the hypothesis is not disproven, but nothing in the evidence supports leading with it. The safer sequencing: launch individual-first (where all the buying evidence is), offer family as the upsell tier competitors already validated, and test family-led messaging as an experiment — measuring CAC *and* per-household servicing cost — before making it the hero. The strongest family-adjacent wedge with actual evidence is ICP-3 (adult children buying scam protection for elderly parents), which is a *gifting* motion, not a 10-user plan.
